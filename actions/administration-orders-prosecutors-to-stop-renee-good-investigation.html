Administration Orders Prosecutors to Stop Renee Good Investigation

January 2026

After the fatal shooting of Renee Good in Minneapolis in January 2026, federal prosecutors began a standard civil rights investigation into a federal immigration agent’s potential use of excessive force. Investigators sought and received a signed warrant to document blood splatter and bullet holes in Good’s car, but were ordered to halt investigative steps by senior administration officials, including FBI Director Kash Patel. According to officials involved, leadership was concerned that a civil rights investigation would contradict President Donald Trump’s public claim that Good “violently, willfully, and viciously ran over the ICE Officer.”

Senior Department of Justice officials instead pushed prosecutors to investigate Good and her family for potential criminal activity. In response, six career prosecutors in the Minnesota U.S. Attorney’s Office resigned. Among them was Joseph Thompson, who had served as acting U.S. attorney for Minnesota in 2025. Thompson reportedly objected strongly to abandoning the civil rights inquiry and to initiating a criminal investigation into Good’s family. Tracee Mergen, supervisor of the FBI’s Minneapolis field office, also resigned following the intervention.

Eyewitness video and testimony from the shooting raised serious factual questions about the agents’ conduct. At minimum, the circumstances appeared to warrant a standard civil rights investigation. The decision to halt that process and redirect prosecutorial resources has prompted allegations of political interference in a potentially unlawful killing of an American citizen.

Potential Legal Issues Raised

1. Obstruction of a Fair and Independent Investigation

If federal officials actively interfered with or impeded a lawful investigation, several federal obstruction statutes could theoretically be implicated:

  • 18 U.S.C. § 1505 – Obstruction of proceedings before departments, agencies, and committees
  • 18 U.S.C. § 1512(c) – Tampering with evidence or otherwise obstructing an official proceeding
  • 18 U.S.C. § 1519 – Destruction, alteration, or concealment of records in a federal investigation

2. Failure to Investigate Possible Civil Rights Violations

  • 18 U.S.C. § 242 – Deprivation of rights under color of law
  • 34 U.S.C. § 12601 – Pattern or practice of conduct depriving persons of constitutional rights

3. Withholding Evidence From State Prosecutors

  • 18 U.S.C. § 1512(b) – Witness tampering
  • 18 U.S.C. § 1519 – Concealment or falsification of records
  • 18 U.S.C. § 4 – Misprision of felony

4. Abuse of Authority or Political Interference

  • 18 U.S.C. § 371 – Conspiracy to defraud the United States
  • 18 U.S.C. § 1503 – Obstruction of justice
  • 5 U.S.C. § 2302(b)(8) – Prohibited personnel practices
  • 28 C.F.R. § 45.2 – DOJ regulation on political conflicts

5. Federalism and State Criminal Jurisdiction

  • In re Neagle (1890) – Federal officer immunity doctrine
  • Supremacy Clause, Article VI of the Constitution
