Cuban Oil Embargo and Risk of Humanitarian Crisis

January 2026

On January 29, 2026, President Donald Trump signed an executive order declaring Cuba an “unusual and extraordinary threat” to United States national security and authorizing tariffs on any country that sells or supplies oil to Cuba. The administration justified the order by asserting that Cuba hosts and supports hostile states and terrorist organizations, including Russia, China, Hamas, and Hezbollah.

These claims were presented as national security justifications despite the absence of publicly verifiable evidence demonstrating that Cuba currently poses such intelligence or security threats to the United States. No intelligence assessments or corroborating documentation were released alongside the order to substantiate the allegations.

The policy directly targets Cuba’s access to energy supplies essential for daily life. Cuba relies on imported oil for approximately eighty percent of its energy consumption. Available data indicate that existing reserves could sustain the country for only fifteen to twenty days at current usage levels if imports are halted. Prolonged power outages have already been reported across the island, with blackouts lasting many hours per day.

As fuel shortages worsen, hospitals, water treatment systems, food distribution networks, transportation, and other critical services face escalating disruption. The United Nations has warned that the humanitarian situation in Cuba could worsen dramatically, if not collapse entirely, if energy supplies are not maintained. Mexican officials, whose country accounts for roughly forty four percent of Cuba’s crude oil imports, have warned that the tariffs could trigger a humanitarian crisis affecting access to food, medicine, and essential services.

The humanitarian consequences would fall overwhelmingly on ordinary civilians rather than on the Cuban government or any alleged foreign adversaries. Even prior to full enforcement of the embargo, food prices have risen, shortages of basic goods have expanded, and daily electricity blackouts have intensified across the country.

The strategy echoes the original logic underpinning early United States embargo policy during the Cold War. A declassified State Department memorandum from April 6, 1960 explicitly stated that the objective of embargo measures was to deny money and supplies to Cuba in order to decrease wages and provoke hunger and desperation as a means of destabilizing the government.

The renewed oil embargo thus combines contested national security claims with a policy that carries a high risk of severe humanitarian harm. Critics argue it continues a decades long embargo strategy that has inflicted substantial economic and social damage on the Cuban population without demonstrating clear benefits to United States security.
