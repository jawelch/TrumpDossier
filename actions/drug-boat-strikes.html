Illegal Boat-Strike Campaign Kills 200+ in the Caribbean

2025-2026

In President Trump's second term, the United States military began conducting airstrikes on boats suspected of transporting drugs into the country. The use of military force is a dramatic escalation unusual for drug enforcement. The administration has argued the vessels belong to 'narco-terrorist' groups that are trafficking fentanyl to the US, justifying lethal force. The government has not released any evidence confirming the groups involved with these boats, identities of the people on board, what drugs they were carrying or if they were drug traffickers at all. The campaign has involved 62 publicly announced strikes and the deaths of at least 200 people.

The campaign has the following international and humanitarian legal issues:

No recognized armed conflict

  • The administration argues it is engaged in an armed conflict against designated "narco-terrorist" organizations.
  • Simply designating a cartel as a terrorist organization does not automatically create an armed conflict under international humanitarian law. If there is no armed conflict, then the law of war generally does not apply, and international human rights law — which imposes a much stricter standard for using lethal force — should govern.

Drug trafficking is traditionally a law enforcement matter

  • For decades, suspected drug-smuggling vessels have typically been intercepted, boarded, searched, and their crews arrested by the Coast Guard or partner nations.
  • Escalating from arrests to airstrikes requires a much stronger legal justification than the administration has publicly provided.

Necessity

  • Even in an armed conflict, lethal force generally must be militarily necessary.
  • The administration has not provided any evidence or rationale for why the boats could not have been intercepted and their crews captured instead of killed.

Distinction

  • The law of armed conflict requires parties to distinguish between lawful military targets and civilians.
  • Because the government has not publicly released the intelligence supporting individual strikes, there is no public evidence that everyone aboard each vessel was a lawful target.

Proportionality

  • Even if a boat is carrying drugs or belongs to a cartel, an attack must not cause excessive civilian harm relative to the anticipated military advantage.
  • Without more information about who was aboard and what intelligence existed, proportionality cannot be independently evaluated.

The "second-strike" incident

  • One of the most controversial reports alleged that a second strike was launched after survivors were already in the water, commonly known as a "double-tap" strike.
  • If those individuals were hors de combat (out of the fight because they were incapacitated or defenseless), intentionally attacking them would violate the law of armed conflict.
Issue Relevant law
1. Whether an armed conflict exists Common Article 3 of the Geneva Conventions (1949) (minimum rules for non-international armed conflicts); Additional Protocol II (not ratified by the U.S., but often cited by scholars); customary international humanitarian law.
2. Law enforcement vs. military force U.N. Charter, Article 2(4) (prohibition on use of force against another state's territory); International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR), Article 6 (right to life); U.N. Basic Principles on the Use of Force and Firearms by Law Enforcement Officials (non-binding standards emphasizing necessity).
3. Necessity DoD Law of War Manual § 2.2 (Military Necessity); customary international humanitarian law (ICRC Rule 14 and related principles).
4. Distinction Additional Protocol I, Article 48 (basic rule of distinction); Articles 51–52 (civilian protection and military objectives); customary international humanitarian law (ICRC Rules 1–10). Although the U.S. is not party to Additional Protocol I, it recognizes many distinction rules as customary law.
5. Proportionality Additional Protocol I, Article 51(5)(b) and Article 57 (proportionality and precautions in attack); customary international humanitarian law (ICRC Rule 14). The U.S. also incorporates proportionality into the DoD Law of War Manual.
6. Second strike / attacking survivors Common Article 3 of the Geneva Conventions (prohibits violence against persons who are hors de combat); Additional Protocol I, Article 41 (defines hors de combat); customary international humanitarian law (ICRC Rule 47). If a person is no longer participating in hostilities and cannot defend themselves, intentionally attacking them is generally prohibited.
