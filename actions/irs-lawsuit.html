Sues His Own Government for $10 Billion, Judge Rules Trump Sought to Extract 'Personal Benefits' and 'Misused Legal System'

July 2026

In January of 2026, President Trump filed a lawsuit against the IRS for the mishandling of his tax records during his first term for $10 billion dollars. Trump's personal lawyers settled with the Justice Department, an entity increasingly under direct control of Trump, in May 2026. Trump dropped the suit in exchange for a $1.776 billion compensation fund — purported to compensate 'political victims' of the government — and sweeping immunity from current, past, and future tax audits of Trump and his family. A federal judge ruled in July 2026 that the settlement was legally invalid and a misuse of the legal system, and referred the lawyers involved for discipline.

Trump's federal tax returns were leaked by an IRS contractor to the New York Times during the 2020 Election campaign. While it is commonplace for political candidates — especially at the presidential level — to publicly disclose financial information for transparency, Trump infamously refused to do so in all of his campaigns. Trump was the first major-party nominee to refuse to disclose his tax returns since before 1976. On this point, Trump is only guilty of breaking precedent, and he does have a valid grievance as the leaking of his tax returns was illegal (the IRS contractor was sentenced to five years in prison). But the leak showed why Trump was keen to keep these records private. Trump's past of incredulous business dealings and potentially inflated self-worth could have discredited the successful billionaire persona he ran his campaigns on. The leaked tax returns showed that Trump paid only $750 in federal income taxes in 2016 and 2017 because of his reported large business losses in recent years. Further examination of Trump's tax record showed much larger potential issues. Trump's claim of a $72.9 million federal tax refund in the early 2000s and other tax benefits over the years led to an audit from the IRS.

These pending issues and the active audit would have been abandoned with the 2026 settlement. If Trump had committed tax fraud or in some other capacity violated tax law, the IRS would not be able to enforce any punishment. If it was concluded that the $72.9 million refund was not appropriate or legitimate, Trump would stand to keep the money regardless. This component of the settlement also raised political issues. Federal law (26 U.S.C. § 7217) bars the President and certain senior executive officials from requesting or directing the IRS to conduct or terminate an audit of a particular taxpayer. The intervention of the Justice Department in an audit is also legally dubious, as Congress has established specific statutory procedures for tax assessments and disputes.

Beyond the legal implications, the settlement was clearly self dealing and an abuse of presidential power. Trump sued his own government for $10 billion dollars, which essentially would have transferred billions of tax payer dollars directly into the president's pockets. Perhaps to reconcile this criticism, the final settlement's financial element was designated as an "anti-weaponization fund" for those who had been subject to alleged politically motivated persecution by the federal government. The administration and oversight of the fund did not provide details about who was eligible to receive compensation from the $1.776 billion fund, nor standards for burden of proof or any legal criteria for "weaponization." The fund could easily be interpreted as a mechanism for Trump to doll out money to his friends and supporters, especially to January 6 defendants who had faced legal action. Capitol police officers from the riot sued the administration to block the fund, as did other parties, and the fund was abandoned due to the extensive criticism.

A federal judge ruled in July that the entire settlement should be considered legally void as the lawsuit had been used for "improper purpose." She concluded that the lawsuit lacked any actual adversarial merit, as the two parties involved and their interests in the 'legal dispute' were essentially the same. Trump controlled both sides of the lawsuit and sought to extract personal benefits from it. In his second term, Trump has gutted the notion of an independent Department of Justice — the acting Attorney General at the time of the settlement was Todd Blanche, Trump's former personal lawyer. The judge imposed disciplinary actions against the attorneys involved in the case and referred Blanche's conduct for review. There is no way to conclude that this lawsuit and settlement were anything but self-serving and a corrupt attempt to pillage the federal government for Trump's own enrichment.
